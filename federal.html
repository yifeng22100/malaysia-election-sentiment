The Dewan Rakyat as GE15 left it

Malaysia's first hung parliament produced a chamber where no bloc could govern alone — which is why the unity government exists at all.

Final composition of the 15th Parliament following the Padang Serai poll deferred to 7 December 2022. "Others" comprises Warisan 3, independents 2, PBM 1 and KDM 1. The governing unity coalition is built from PH, BN, GPS and GRS — a combination that clears the 112-seat threshold only by spanning parties that contested against each other. Seat counts have shifted since with by-elections and realignments; see the Parliament page for how the chamber works.

Who actually voted: the youth-turnout picture

Malaysia's Election Commission doesn't publish official turnout broken down by age or ethnicity — what follows is academic analysis of the EC's own roll and results data, attributed to the researcher who did it, not this site's own figures.

Professional research

GE15 (2022) turnout was 74% overall. Political scientist Dr Bridget Welsh's analysis of the Election Commission's GE15 results sheet put turnout among 18–30-year-olds at 75.6% — essentially matching the 41–50 age bracket — with a clear gradient inside that band: 79% among 18–20-year-olds (the first cohort to vote under Undi18's lowered voting age), 76% among 21–25s, and 72% among 26–30s. Youth turnout was not dramatically lower than older voters, the pattern often assumed before the count.

Scale matters here as much as the rate: 18–39-year-olds made up 50.3% of the entire GE15 electoral roll, split roughly into 18–20s (6.5%), 21–29s (21.7%) and 30–39s (22.1%) — largely the product of Undi18's automatic voter registration, which added an estimated 5.8 million voters to the roll in one move (see the Election Commission section). That share isn't even across states: Welsh's figures put youth voters at 33% of the roll in Kelantan and Terengganu, 32% in Selangor, 30% in Kedah, 27% in Negeri Sembilan and 25% in Penang — which is part of why several of this site's not-yet-due state pages flag youth turnout as a factor to watch.

On ethnicity specifically: unlike age, Malaysia's electoral roll and ballot data don't carry a voter's ethnicity, so any "ethnic voting" analysis (including Welsh's own separate work on the topic) is inferred from how constituencies with different ethnic compositions voted, not from individual ballots — a meaningfully weaker form of evidence than the age figures above, which come directly from the roll's registered birth dates.

GE15 by state

The national 82/74/30 split hides very different state-level pictures — PN's strength is concentrated in the Malay-belt north and east coast, PH's in the urban west coast and Sabah/Sarawak lean on their own regional coalitions.

Reading sentiment: two very different sources

This site draws on two kinds of sentiment signal, and keeps them visually distinct rather than blending them into one number.

Professional research — a named pollster's methodology (sample size, dates, sampling frame) is disclosed.
Public / social reaction — what's being said on Facebook, X, TikTok, Instagram or forums, as reported by news outlets covering that reaction. This site does not scrape social platforms directly; it relies on press coverage of what's trending, exactly as it does for every other signal-log entry.

The professional pollsters this site cites

Merdeka Center for Opinion Research — Malaysia's longest-running independent pollster, based in Bangi, a founding member of the Malaysian Market Research Association and a member of WAPOR (the World Association for Public Opinion Research). Uses stratified random sampling with in-person and phone interviews, weighted to demographic and ethnic composition. Its most recent national survey (12 March – 9 April 2026, 1,209 registered voters across all 14 states and all 222 parliamentary constituencies) put PM Anwar Ibrahim's approval at 52%, UMNO's Khairy Jamaluddin at 50%, and found the country split on direction: 42% right track, 50% wrong track — essentially unchanged since December 2025.

Ilham Centre — conducts face-to-face interviews rather than phone or online polling, on the stated view that phone surveys understate accuracy; its Johor and Negeri Sembilan pre-election projections are cited on those state pages.

Vodus Research — a newer entrant using an online "one-question" ad-embedded survey technology (OMTOS) rather than a traditional panel; also cited on the Johor and Negeri Sembilan pages for its pre-election forecasts.

All three disclose sample size and fieldwork dates for the specific polls this site cites — a bar social-media sentiment cannot meet, which is exactly why the two are labelled separately rather than presented as equivalent.

A year after leaving Cabinet, Rafizi Ramli and Nik Nazmi Nik Ahmad announced they would quit PKR entirely and vacate their seats (Pandan and Setiawangsa) to take over Parti Bersama Malaysia, a small existing party — a deeper rupture in the unity coalition than their 2025 Cabinet resignations. Coalition realignment

PAS formally ends cooperation with Bersatu, fracturing Perikatan Nasional ahead of GE16

PAS's Central Working Committee voted to end the party's political cooperation with Bersatu, marking the most significant rupture inside the opposition Perikatan Nasional coalition since its founding, over irreconcilable differences on the future direction of opposition politics and efforts to broaden Malay-Muslim political unity — tensions analysts trace partly to disputes following the December 2025 Perlis political crisis, which PAS explicitly named as a trigger. Bersatu said it intends to remain a PN component party regardless of the split; PAS-led Kelantan and Terengganu both said their state governments would carry on unchanged.

Legislation — failed

Bill to cap any PM's cumulative tenure at 10 years fails to pass

The Constitution (Amendment) Bill 2026 would have capped any individual's cumulative time as Prime Minister at 10 years, applied retroactively to past and current PMs. It needed a two-thirds majority (148 votes) to pass; it received only 146 in favour at second reading, with 44 abstentions and 32 absent — a genuinely significant near-miss reform that fell two votes short.

Bill to split the Attorney-General and Public Prosecutor roles remains pending

The Constitution (Amendment) (No. 2) Bill 2026 would separate the Attorney-General and Public Prosecutor positions and strengthen Parliament's role in scrutinising the Public Prosecutor appointment. Referred to a Special Select Committee, whose report was tabled 22 June 2026; second reading, originally set for 13 July, was postponed to let the government present the amendments to the Conference of Rulers first. As of this page's most recent check, it had not yet returned to the Dewan Rakyat for a final vote.

Major Cabinet reshuffle: over 25 positions changed

Anwar reshuffled more than 25 Cabinet positions — several ministerial portfolio realignments plus new ministerial and deputy-minister appointments. Zaliha Mustafa (Minister in the PM's Department for Federal Territories) and Mohd Na'im Mokhtar (Minister in the PM's Department for religious affairs) were both dropped from Cabinet. Legislation

Parliamentary Services Act 2025 passed, restoring Parliament's institutional independence

A constitutional amendment clearing the way passed 4 March 2025; the Parliamentary Services Bill itself passed the Dewan Rakyat the next day (148 in favour, 57 abstained, 17 absent). It re-establishes Parliament's own administrative service separate from the civil service, reviving the Parliamentary Services Act 1966 that had been repealed in 1992 — a significant institutional-independence reform.

Cabinet resignations

Rafizi Ramli and Nik Nazmi resign from Cabinet after PKR election losses; Tengku Zafrul quits UMNO

Economy Minister Rafizi Ramli and Natural Resources Minister Nik Nazmi Nik Ahmad both resigned after losing internal PKR party elections — Rafizi lost the deputy presidency to Nurul Izzah Anwar, Nik Nazmi lost a vice-presidency race — widely read as fallout from grassroots discontent inside PKR. The Dewan Rakyat narrowly passed the Communications and Multimedia (Amendment) Bill 2024 via a bloc vote after an Opposition walkout, expanding CMA 1998 enforcement powers — criticised by ARTICLE 19 as "a step backwards for freedom of expression." Legislation

Abolition of Mandatory Death Penalty Act 2023 passed

A landmark reform removing the mandatory death penalty for a range of offences — including drug trafficking and murder — giving judges sentencing discretion instead, with a companion Act letting existing death-row and life prisoners apply for resentencing. Passed the Dewan Rakyat 3 April, the Dewan Negara 11 April, received royal assent in June and came into force 4 July 2023.

What it adds up to

Two decided Peninsular elections, two losses for PH — through different mechanisms, but the same direction.

Johor and Negeri Sembilan broke against PH via genuinely different routes: Johor through PN sitting out a third of the seats and Malay voters consolidating behind BN; Negeri Sembilan through a formal BN–PN non-compete pact that split all 36 seats between the two non-PH blocs and ran them as one anti-PH front. Different mechanisms, same outcome — and Negeri Sembilan carried a heavier personal cost, with PH losing its own state chairman (Menteri Besar Aminuddin Harun) and DAP secretary-general Anthony Loke in the same night.

For a federal unity government built on PH, BN, GPS and GRS all sitting in the same cabinet, the state-level story is really a story about UMNO's (and now the BN–PN pact's) internal leverage. A second win validates the non-compete model as repeatable rather than a one-off, which increases pressure on PH to recalibrate before facing voters nationally, and raises the political cost of Anwar's coalition partners drifting further from PH's own positioning. None of this forces an early dissolution — the unity government's parliamentary arithmetic hasn't changed — but it shapes the environment GE16 will eventually be fought in.

Sabah sits outside this reading. Its November 2025 hung assembly and GRS-led coalition government reflect Sabah's own multi-party dynamics rather than the Peninsula's BN/PH/PN framing — DAP's total wipeout there says more about Sabah-specific alignment than about federal sentiment.

Two results in, the consequences are starting to reach the unity government's own component parties. DAP's central executive committee has called a special national congress for 16 August, putting a secret-ballot motion to roughly 4,000 delegates on whether the party — the largest in PH's federal bloc, with five ministers and seven deputy ministers in Anwar's cabinet — should stay in government for the rest of the term. Secretary-general Anthony Loke says the outcome won't change DAP's support for Anwar or its place in PH, but the vote itself, coming three weeks after Loke lost his own Negeri Sembilan seat, is a direct read on how much the two defeats have shaken the party's base. Delegates ultimately backed staying by an 88% margin — 1,857 for to 242 against, of 2,099 valid votes — giving Loke and DAP's ministers and deputy ministers a clear mandate to remain in cabinet for the rest of the term. Separately, PBM president and Julau MP Larry Sng argued a consolidating BN–PN pact — which he noted already held a combined 104 seats after GE15 — could reach a simple federal majority after GE16 without needing Sabah or Sarawak's coalitions at all; GPS figures in both East Malaysian states publicly disputed the idea, but the fact it's being argued in public is itself a sign of how far the BN–PN axis's confidence has travelled since Johor.

Anwar himself has struck a conciliatory note in public since the Negeri Sembilan defeat. Speaking at National Landscape Day on 7 August, he said "political differences must not obstruct cooperation" between federal and state governments where public welfare is at stake, and that differing party affiliations "must not hinder friendship, unity, and cooperation that directly benefit the people" — a pointed message given BN and PN now jointly control two of the three decided states while remaining, respectively, a coalition partner and the main opposition at federal level.

What we're watching next
Resolved: DAP's 16 August congress

Delegates voted 1,857 to 242 (88%) to keep DAP in Anwar's unity government for the rest of the term — a clear mandate for secretary-general Anthony Loke and the party's five ministers and seven deputy ministers to stay in cabinet, and evidence the two state losses haven't fractured DAP's base as sharply as some had expected.

Watch: Melaka & Sarawak

Both remaining anticipated states — Melaka and Sarawak — are still undated, but expected to firm up now that Johor and Negeri Sembilan have landed. Neither is likely to change the federal arithmetic directly, but each adds another data point to the pattern this page is tracking. The eight states not yet due don't face elections until 2027–2028 at the earliest, putting them on a similar clock to GE16 itself.

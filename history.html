The Election Commission & Malaysia's electoral history

Role & functions

The Suruhanjaya Pilihan Raya (SPR) — the Election Commission — is the constitutional body responsible for running federal and state elections in Malaysia. Article 113 of the Federal Constitution gives it three core jobs: compiling and revising the electoral roll, studying and redelineating parliamentary and state constituency boundaries, and conducting general elections, state elections and by-elections. It sits administratively under the Prime Minister's Department, though its decisions are meant to be made independently of the executive.

Its seven members — a chairman, a deputy chairman and five other commissioners — are appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong (the King), and Article 114(1) requires that this happen only after consultation with the Conference of Rulers, a safeguard intended to keep the appointment process above ordinary party politics. In practice, appointments are made on the advice of the Prime Minister, which is the crux of most independence debates about the body.

Independence & controversy

Because commissioners are appointed on the sitting government's recommendation, critics — most prominently the electoral-reform coalition Bersih — have long argued the EC's independence is structurally compromised, regardless of who happens to hold power. The clearest flashpoint has been redelineation: the constituency boundary review approved just before GE14 in 2018 was widely criticised as rushed and favourable to the then-ruling Barisan Nasional, reviving long-standing complaints about malapportionment (constituencies of very unequal population size) and gerrymandering (boundaries drawn to favour a particular party). Bersih has continued to push for EC appointments to be vetted by a parliamentary special select committee rather than decided solely within the executive, a reform promised by successive governments but not yet delivered in full.

Recent reforms

The most significant change to how Malaysians vote in decades came from Undi18: a constitutional amendment passed unanimously by Parliament in July 2019 that lowered the voting age from 21 to 18 and introduced automatic voter registration (AVR), enrolling every eligible citizen without requiring them to apply. Implementation was delayed for two years — the EC initially said it could not be ready before September 2022 — until a Kuching High Court ruling in September 2022 ordered the government to act. In practice, both changes had already taken effect on 15 December 2021, and GE15 in November 2022 was the first general election run under the lower voting age and automatic rolls, adding an estimated 5.8 million new and younger voters to the electorate.

Why the boundaries on this site's maps keep changing

Every map on this site is dated, because the lines it draws aren't permanent — Malaysia's constituencies are periodically redrawn, and the boundaries in force at any given election shape the result as much as the votes do.

Two different problems, one word people use for both

Malapportionment is when constituencies hold very different numbers of voters — a 15,000-voter rural seat and a 150,000-voter urban seat each still elect one representative, so a rural vote counts for roughly ten times as much as an urban one. It doesn't require any bad-faith line-drawing at all; it can simply be old boundaries that never caught up with where people moved. Malaysia's Federal Constitution actually permits some malapportionment by design — a legacy provision meant to protect rural representation — which is unusually explicit compared to most democracies.

Gerrymandering is the more deliberate cousin: drawing boundaries specifically to pack a rival's voters into few seats or spread them thinly across many, so their votes translate into fewer seats won than their vote share would suggest. Academic analysis of Malaysia's 2003 and 2016–18 redelineation exercises has found evidence of both problems compounding each other, with rural, then-BN-leaning seats systematically smaller than urban opposition-leaning ones.

The redelineation timeline behind this site's own maps

Article 113 requires the Election Commission to review constituency boundaries at least once every eight years. In practice:

  • 2003 (Peninsular, Labuan, Sabah) / 2005 (Sarawak) — the boundaries used through GE12 (2008) and GE13 (2013), the exercise most cited in the malapportionment research above.
  • 2016–2018 — approved just months before GE14, widely criticised (including by Bersih and independent researchers) as rushed and skewed to benefit the then-governing Barisan Nasional. BN lost GE14 anyway.
  • Sabah, 2019 — a separate post-GE14 exercise specific to Sabah, expanding it from 60 to 73 state seats and adjusting its 25 federal seats.
  • Sarawak — still runs on its 2015 boundaries; the state assembly has approved an expansion from 82 to 99 seats, but as of this page's last update it isn't yet reflected in a completed redelineation (see the Sarawak page).

This is exactly why every map on this site states its delimitation year in the caption underneath it: the Peninsular boundaries on the federal map are from 2018, Sabah's from 2019, Sarawak's from 2015 — three different vintages stitched into one national picture, because that's genuinely how current Malaysian electoral geography works.

Going deeper than this site does

Building and maintaining boundary-comparison maps (showing exactly which areas moved between one delimitation and the next) is a serious cartographic undertaking on its own — this site uses the boundaries as delivered by ElectionData.MY rather than reconstructing that history. Tindak Malaysia's Redelineation Through Visuals project has done exactly that work, and is the most thorough public resource on how Malaysia's constituencies have actually shifted over time.

How Malaysia's elections have evolved — 1955 to today

Sixteen general elections, one racial crisis that suspended Parliament, one constitutional amendment that added millions of voters, and — after 61 years of unbroken rule by one coalition — two changes of federal government within four years.

Same colours, different names: a coalition family tree

Every rebrand this site's maps and charts treat as "one continuous thread" — Barisan Alternatif becoming Pakatan Rakyat becoming Pakatan Harapan, for instance — actually happened. Here's when, and what else was splitting or merging at the same time. Not to scale; positioned for legibility, not exact year-proportional width.

Alliance → Barisan Nasional (1974): UMNO, MCA and MIC's Alliance expanded into the larger, still-governing BN coalition after the 1969 riots and a wave of opposition parties joining the government. Gagasan Rakyat → Barisan Alternatif → Pakatan Rakyat → Pakatan Harapan: the DAP/PKR-led opposition's name has changed four times since 1990 without ever really stopping — GR (1990) and the PAS-led APU ran as separate opposition blocs that election, merged into one Barisan Alternatif for 1999–2008 after the Reformasi movement, formalised as Pakatan Rakyat in 2008, then reconstituted as Pakatan Harapan in 2015 after PAS left over DAP tensions (Amanah, a PAS splinter, joined PH instead). The Sheraton Move (2020): Bersatu, part of the PH government since GE14, defected along with a bloc of PKR MPs, collapsing the PH federal government; Bersatu then joined PAS to form Perikatan Nasional. That's why PAS runs alone in this diagram from 2015–2020, then reappears inside PN. GPS and GRS aren't rebrands of anything — Sarawak's BN component parties split off to govern independently as GPS after GE14 (2018), and Sabah's GRS formed fresh for that state's 2020 election. Both now sit in the federal unity government alongside PH and BN. Socialist Front (1957–1969): the earliest organised opposition, dissolved after the state of emergency following the 13 May 1969 riots; no later party claims direct descent from it.

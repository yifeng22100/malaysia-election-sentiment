The Election Commission & Malaysia's electoral history

Who runs Malaysia's elections, how that body is meant to work, and how the country's vote has evolved from the first ballot in 1955 to the rolling 2025–2028 state election cycle this site tracks.

Role & functions

The Suruhanjaya Pilihan Raya (SPR) — the Election Commission — is the constitutional body responsible for running federal and state elections in Malaysia. Article 113 of the Federal Constitution gives it three core jobs: compiling and revising the electoral roll, studying and redelineating parliamentary and state constituency boundaries, and conducting general elections, state elections and by-elections. It sits administratively under the Prime Minister's Department, though its decisions are meant to be made independently of the executive.

Its seven members — a chairman, a deputy chairman and five other commissioners — are appointed by the Yang di-Pertuan Agong (the King), and Article 114(1) requires that this happen only after consultation with the Conference of Rulers, a safeguard intended to keep the appointment process above ordinary party politics. In practice, appointments are made on the advice of the Prime Minister, which is the crux of most independence debates about the body.

Independence & controversy

Because commissioners are appointed on the sitting government's recommendation, critics — most prominently the electoral-reform coalition Bersih — have long argued the EC's independence is structurally compromised, regardless of who happens to hold power. The clearest flashpoint has been redelineation: the constituency boundary review approved just before GE14 in 2018 was widely criticised as rushed and favourable to the then-ruling Barisan Nasional, reviving long-standing complaints about malapportionment (constituencies of very unequal population size) and gerrymandering (boundaries drawn to favour a particular party). Bersih has continued to push for EC appointments to be vetted by a parliamentary special select committee rather than decided solely within the executive, a reform promised by successive governments but not yet delivered in full.

Recent reforms

The most significant change to how Malaysians vote in decades came from Undi18: a constitutional amendment passed unanimously by Parliament in July 2019 that lowered the voting age from 21 to 18 and introduced automatic voter registration (AVR), enrolling every eligible citizen without requiring them to apply. Implementation was delayed for two years — the EC initially said it could not be ready before September 2022 — until a Kuching High Court ruling in September 2022 ordered the government to act. In practice, both changes had already taken effect on 15 December 2021, and GE15 in November 2022 was the first general election run under the lower voting age and automatic rolls, adding an estimated 5.8 million new and younger voters to the electorate.

How Malaysia's elections have evolved — 1955 to today

Seventeen general elections, one racial crisis that suspended Parliament, one constitutional amendment that added millions of voters, and — after 61 years of unbroken rule by one coalition — two changes of federal government within four years.

27 Jul 1955
Founding vote

Malaya's first general election — the only one before independence

The Alliance (UMNO, MCA and MIC) won 51 of 52 federal legislative council seats and roughly 80% of the vote. Its leader, Tunku Abdul Rahman, became Chief Minister and led Malaya to independence two years later — establishing the communal-coalition template that Malaysian politics has run on ever since.

Wikipedia — 1955 Malayan general election

31 Aug 1957
Institution

Merdeka, and the Election Commission is established

Independence brought a written constitution with the Election Commission built into it from day one — established 4 September 1957, days after Merdeka — tasked with running every federal and state election from then on under Articles 113–120.

SPR Portal — history of the Election Commission

10 May 1969
Crisis

Alliance loses its two-thirds majority; riots suspend Parliament for nearly two years

The Alliance retained a simple majority but lost its two-thirds supermajority as DAP and Gerakan made major gains among non-Malay voters. Racial riots broke out in Kuala Lumpur on 13 May, days after the results. The Yang di-Pertuan Agong declared a state of emergency; Parliament and constitutional government were suspended and power passed to the all-powerful National Operations Council until Parliament reconvened in February 1971.

Wikipedia — 13 May incident

1974
Realignment

Barisan Nasional formed from the wreckage of 1969

The government's response to 1969 was to broaden the ruling coalition rather than narrow it: PAS joined in 1973, and by 1974 the two-party Alliance had been reconstituted as the much larger, multi-party Barisan Nasional, which went on to contest and win every general election for the next 44 years.

1990
Internal challenge

Semangat 46's Team B challenge — the most serious internal threat to BN before 2018

Tengku Razaleigh Hamzah's Semangat 46, born from a bitterly contested 1987 UMNO leadership defeat, allied with PAS and DAP to challenge Mahathir Mohamad's BN. It failed to unseat the government, and most of its members had rejoined UMNO by 1996 — but it previewed the coalition-realignment and party-hopping patterns that would recur in 2018 and 2020.

See the Parties page for Semangat 46's full history

29 Nov 1999
Reformasi

The Reformasi election — Barisan Alternatif's high-water mark for PAS

Held a year after Anwar Ibrahim's dismissal and imprisonment sparked the Reformasi protest movement, this was the first election contested by a formal opposition pact — Barisan Alternatif (PAS, DAP and the newly formed Keadilan). BN retained power comfortably, 148 of 193 seats, but PAS more than tripled its own seat count from 7 to 27 and captured both Kelantan and Terengganu, its strongest showing yet.

Wikipedia — 1999 Malaysian general election

8 Mar 2008
Turning point

The "political tsunami" — BN loses its two-thirds majority for the first time since 1969

BN won a fresh mandate, 140 of 222 seats, but lost its two-thirds supermajority and, more strikingly, control of five states — Penang, Selangor, Kedah and Perak newly, plus Kelantan retained by PAS — as the opposition nearly quadrupled its seat count to 82. Local media dubbed it a "political tsunami," and it marked the start of a 14-year decline that ended BN's federal rule a decade later.

Malaysiakini — the day we surfed a tsunami

5 May 2013
Popular vote

BN keeps power despite losing the popular vote for the first time

Pakatan Rakyat (PAS, DAP and PKR) won 50.9% of the national vote to BN's 47.4% — but Malaysia's first-past-the-post, malapportioned constituencies gave BN 133 seats to PR's 89, enough for Najib Razak to secure a second term as prime minister despite trailing in votes cast, intensifying redelineation and electoral-reform debates.

Wikipedia — 2013 Malaysian general election

9 May 2018
Historic

GE14 — the first change of federal government since independence

Pakatan Harapan, led by a 92-year-old Mahathir Mohamad now allied with his former rival Anwar Ibrahim, won 113 of 222 seats to BN's 79, ending 61 unbroken years of Alliance/BN federal rule — one of the longest single-party dominance runs in the democratic world at the time it ended. PAS took 18 seats, Parti Warisan Sabah 8, cementing Sabah's status as a genuine third force outside the Peninsula's coalition framework.

Wikipedia — 2018 Malaysian general election

Feb 2020
Collapse

The Sheraton Move topples PH — without an election

Bersatu and a faction of PKR MPs defected from the governing Pakatan Harapan coalition, collapsing Malaysia's first-ever change-of-government administration less than two years into its term. Muhyiddin Yassin became prime minister via new coalition arithmetic in Parliament rather than a fresh mandate from voters — a reminder that a government's fall doesn't always run through the ballot box.

See the Parties page for Bersatu's role in the Sheraton Move

15 Dec 2021
Reform

Undi18 takes effect: voting age drops to 18, registration goes automatic

A constitutional amendment passed unanimously in 2019 finally took effect after a two-year implementation delay: the voting age fell from 21 to 18, and automatic voter registration enrolled every eligible citizen without an application step — the biggest expansion of Malaysia's electorate since independence, adding an estimated 5.8 million voters ahead of GE15.

ConstitutionNet — bipartisan support for lowering the voting age

19 Nov 2022
Historic

GE15 — Malaysia's first-ever hung parliament

No coalition came close to a majority: Pakatan Harapan led with 82 seats, Perikatan Nasional surged to 73, and BN suffered the worst result in its history, just 30 of the 178 seats it contested. Five days of uncertainty followed before the King appointed Anwar Ibrahim prime minister at the head of a multi-coalition unity government spanning PH, BN, GPS and GRS — a novel arrangement this site's federal page continues to track.

Current cycle

The rolling 2025–2028 state election cycle — this site's subject

Rather than one nationwide vote, Malaysia's 13 state assemblies each run on their own five-year clock, producing a staggered sequence of state elections between Sabah's vote in November 2025 and the next general election due by early 2028. This is the story this whole site exists to follow — see the Overview page for where things currently stand.

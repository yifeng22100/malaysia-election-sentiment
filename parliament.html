Parliament: Dewan Rakyat & Dewan Negara

Malaysia's Parliament has two chambers — one elected, one appointed. Here is who sits in each, how a bill becomes law between them, and the decisions taken in that building that reshaped the country since 1957.

The two chambers

Parliament consists of the Yang di-Pertuan Agong and two Houses. Only one of them is elected by voters — the other exists to slow things down and to represent the states.

What the Dewan Rakyat does

The elected chamber is where governments are made and unmade. Every member represents one geographic constituency, drawn by the Election Commission to be as equal in population as practicable — a standard critics argue has been unevenly applied. The chamber's core powers are to legislate, to authorise all government spending and taxation, and to hold the executive to account through questions, debates and select committees.

Crucially, it is also the chamber that determines who governs: a Prime Minister holds office only while commanding the confidence of a majority of its members. Money Bills — anything authorising taxation or expenditure — can only originate here, and only a Minister may introduce one.

What the Dewan Negara does — and what it can't

The Senate is a revising chamber. It reviews bills passed by the Dewan Rakyat, debates them, and can propose amendments or delay them — but it cannot ultimately block them. If the Senate rejects or fails to pass an ordinary bill, the Dewan Rakyat can send it for royal assent after a one-year delay; for a Money Bill, the delay is just one month. It cannot originate Money Bills at all.

Its design purpose is twofold: to give the 13 states a direct voice in federal lawmaking through their two elected senators each, and to bring people into Parliament whom the electoral system would not produce — minority-community representatives, and professionals with specialist expertise. Because senators need not be elected, the chamber is also used to bring ministers into the government who did not win, or did not contest, a Dewan Rakyat seat.

Reform debate centres on the imbalance: 44 of 70 seats are effectively in the Prime Minister's gift, which critics argue undercuts the chamber's federal purpose and its independence as a check on the lower house.

How a bill becomes law
First reading — Dewan Rakyat

The bill is formally introduced and its title read. There is no debate at this stage; the bill is printed and circulated to members.

Second reading

The substantive debate on the bill's principle and purpose. This is where a bill is politically won or lost, and where the opposition makes its case against it.

Committee stage

The bill is examined clause by clause and amendments can be moved and voted on individually.

Third reading

A final vote on the bill as amended. Ordinary bills need a simple majority; constitutional amendments need a two-thirds majority — which is why losing that threshold has always been such a significant political event.

Dewan Negara

The bill repeats the process in the Senate. If passed, it proceeds to royal assent; if rejected, the delay rules above apply rather than an outright veto.

Royal assent & gazetting

The bill goes to the Yang di-Pertuan Agong, who has 30 days to assent. If he does not act within that window, the bill becomes law anyway — an amendment made in 1994 that removed the monarch's ability to block legislation indefinitely. It then takes effect on publication in the Gazette.

Landmark decisions since independence

The Constitution has been amended more than 60 times since 1957. These are the parliamentary decisions that most changed how Malaysia is governed — in both directions.

1960
Security

Internal Security Act 1960

Enacted as the Malayan Emergency wound down, the ISA permitted indefinite detention without trial. It outlived the insurgency it was written for by half a century and became the single most contested law in Malaysian politics — used against communists, but later also against opposition politicians, activists and religious figures.

1971
Economy & speech

The New Economic Policy, and the post-1969 constitutional amendments

After Parliament resumed in February 1971 following the 13 May 1969 riots, it enacted the framework that defined modern Malaysia: the NEP, a 20-year affirmative-action programme to eradicate poverty and restructure economic imbalance between ethnic groups, alongside amendments making it seditious to publicly question the "sensitive issues" — citizenship, Malay special position, the national language and the rulers' sovereignty — even in Parliament itself.

1988
Judiciary

Article 121 amended — judicial power subordinated to Parliament

Amid the fallout from the 1987 UMNO leadership crisis, Article 121 was amended so that the courts held only such jurisdiction as federal law conferred, rather than inherent "judicial power of the Federation." Lord President Salleh Abas and two Supreme Court judges were removed. In 2017 the Federal Court held that the 1988 amendment was unconstitutional, though it stopped short of striking the Act down. It remains the reference point in every debate about judicial independence in Malaysia.

1993
Rulers

Rulers' legal immunity removed

Parliament amended the Constitution to strip the Rulers of blanket immunity from prosecution, creating a Special Court to hear cases involving them. It was a significant assertion of parliamentary authority over the traditional monarchy, passed after a high-profile assault case involving a member of a royal family.

2009
Anti-corruption

The Malaysian Anti-Corruption Commission replaces the ACA

Legislated at the end of Abdullah Badawi's term and operational from 2009, the MACC gave Malaysia's anti-corruption body wider powers and a more institutionalised structure, with oversight panels intended to insulate it from political interference — a structure whose independence has been debated in every administration since.

2012
Security

ISA repealed, SOSMA enacted

Najib Razak's government repealed the Internal Security Act and replaced it with the Security Offences (Special Measures) Act 2012, which abolished indefinite detention without trial but retained a 28-day detention power and special trial procedures. Human-rights organisations characterised it as a narrowing rather than an end of the old regime, and SOSMA's repeal remains a live demand.

2015 → 2018
Taxation

GST introduced, then abolished within three years

A 6% Goods and Services Tax took effect in April 2015, broadening the government's revenue base but proving deeply unpopular amid rising living costs. Abolishing it was a central Pakatan Harapan election promise in 2018 — delivered in September 2018 with a return to the narrower Sales and Service Tax. It remains one of the clearest cases of an election directly reversing a major fiscal policy.

2019 → 2021
Franchise

Undi18 — voting age lowered to 18, automatic registration introduced

Passed unanimously in July 2019 — 211 votes to zero in the Dewan Rakyat — the amendment lowered the voting age from 21 and made voter registration automatic. Implementation was delayed for two years until a court order and political pressure forced it through in December 2021, adding an estimated 5.8 million voters. First proposed by Lim Kit Siang in 1971, it took 48 years to become law.

2022
Stability

Anti-hopping law — Article 49A

A direct legislative response to the 2020 Sheraton Move, passed with cross-party support under the Ismail Sabri government's memorandum of understanding with Pakatan Harapan. MPs who leave the party they were elected under now lose their seat. Also first proposed by Lim Kit Siang — in 1978, 44 years earlier.
